Straight out of college, I was enthusiastic (and still am 😁) about the entire software engineering domain and had a lot of exuberance within me, which I wanted to channel towards building great software.

But all I had done, was a lot of Data Structure and Algorithm problems. I loved solving challenging intellectual problems and solved around 400 to 500 problems across several competitive coding websites for fun.

I thought software engineering was like a coding contest where you solve problems by implementing complex algorithms.

I could not have been more wrong.

The world of Software Engineering had me amazed. Here are some observations I had and the things I learned along the way:

1. It is not that Software Engineering is not challenging. It is just **challenging in a different way**. You would need to figure out how different systems interact to deliver a business use case, what services owned by other teams need to make changes, and whether the software passes quality tests, performance tests, regression tests, behavioural tests and much more.
2. **You WILL get stuck** and you need to figure your way out of that. Sometimes it would be a technical problem, while sometimes it would be a non-technical issue such as approaching a different team or having manager 1:1s.
3. You might get **intimidated** by the breadth of things out there. I was. Take a breather. You don’t need to know everything upfront. Nobody is quizzing you. Just try to get good at collating and learning stuff you don’t know about.
4. I failed a lot. In fact, I always keep a resume of my failures handy in my Notion database. It helps me to know what things I should not do again. What helped me is to be a **feedback sponge**. It is okay to make mistakes. Be humble. And strive to be better each day. Excellence will follow.
5. It helped me to **work from the office**. I listened carefully to discussions around me, especially when they covered topics I wasn’t familiar with. This way I always had a backlog of things to learn.
6. Lastly, being with a buddy or a mentor goes a long way. I am lucky I found one early on. **A good mentor will be the catalyst you need** in your journey, no matter what domain you are in.

I feel like these lessons have shaped me to be a somewhat better engineer.

I hoped that some of you would be able to benefit from these as well.

This is a series I am starting for each of the last six years. And, there are a bunch of more interesting lessons coming up.
